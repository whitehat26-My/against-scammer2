## How it starts

The first message arrives unrequested. It is friendly, short, and offers side work you can do from your phone: boosting hotel ratings, liking videos, placing “orders” in an online shop, or reviewing data.

If you reply, you are added to a Telegram group. The group looks busy — a supervisor, other workers asking questions, screenshots of payments landing. Almost all of them are accounts the syndicate controls.

The first few tasks really are paid. You might receive RM30 or RM80 in your bank account within minutes. That payment is a marketing cost for the syndicate, and it works: after receiving money once, most people stop being critical.

Then comes the “high-value task” that requires you to load your own capital. After that, the dashboard shows a growing balance, but every withdrawal attempt produces a new condition.

## Why it works

The model runs on the same principle as a slot machine: small, unpredictable rewards early, then losses that arrive after you have already invested time and money.

It also uses social pressure. Inside the group, everyone else appears to be succeeding. If you hesitate, a private message from the “supervisor” will convince you the problem is on your side.

More dangerously, some of these offers are really mule account recruitment. Victims who hand over their bank accounts can face investigation and account freezes, even though they were deceived too.

## How to protect yourself

The simplest rule: **a real employer pays you; you never pay an employer.** Any “job” that requires you to load capital, pay a deposit, or buy a membership package is not a job.

Check the company that claims to be hiring. Search the name on the SSM register and see whether an official website and email domain exist. Real recruiters use a company email domain, not Gmail.

Never open a bank account for someone else, hand over your ATM card, or let your account be used to receive third-party money. Legally, it is the account holder who has to answer for it.
