## How it starts

Most cases begin with a social media advert or an invitation into a WhatsApp or Telegram group. The advert usually uses the face of someone you recognise — a politician, a well-known businessperson, a news presenter — with a quote they never said.

Inside the group, a “mentor” gives daily instructions and other members report their gains. You are encouraged to start small. The platform looks professional: moving charts, a balance rising each day, a withdrawal button that works.

That small first withdrawal usually succeeds. It is the most important step in their script, because it converts your doubt into confidence. After that you are pushed to add much larger sums — sometimes retirement savings or a personal loan.

When you request a large withdrawal, new conditions appear. Tax. Penalties. A VIP account activation fee. Each one must be paid in before money can come out.

## Why it works

The balance on that platform is a number, not money. A number can say anything. But when we watch a balance rise daily, our brains treat it as real wealth, and giving it up hurts more than the risk of losing more.

Syndicates also cut you off from outside advice. Conversations happen in closed groups with private messages from the mentor. Anyone who questions things is removed before others can read it.

## How to protect yourself

Check the licence before you send money. The Securities Commission Malaysia publishes lists of licensed parties and an investor alert list; Bank Negara publishes the Financial Consumer Alert list. If the company is not licensed, there is nothing further to discuss.

Watch where the money goes. Legitimate financial companies do not ask for transfers into personal bank accounts or individual crypto wallets.

Test a meaningful withdrawal before adding capital, and stop entirely the moment any fee is demanded before a withdrawal. That is the decisive sign, not an administrative hurdle.
