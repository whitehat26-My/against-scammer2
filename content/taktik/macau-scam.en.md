## How it starts

Most cases start with an automated recorded call. The voice is calm and formal: a parcel is being held, a summons is unpaid, a bank account has been opened in your name in another state. You are asked to press a number to speak to an officer.

From there you enter a carefully staged chain of calls. The first person plays a courier or bank employee. The second is a “police officer”. Sometimes there is a third — a “prosecutor” or “Bank Negara officer”. Each transfer increases your trust, because it feels like you are moving up through an official system.

The most unsettling part is how accurate their details are. They may recite your full name and IC number. That information comes from data leaks, not police records — but in the moment it feels like proof that they really are the authorities.

It all ends with the same request: move your money to a “safe account” so it can be verified as clean.

## Why it works

The syndicate uses sustained pressure and isolation. You are told to stay on the line, tell no one, and act now. As long as you never get a chance to stop and ask someone else, their script is never tested.

They also use fear of authority. Many people who have never dealt with the police do not know the real procedure, so they treat strange instructions as part of a legal process they simply do not understand.

## How to protect yourself

Remember one fact that resolves almost every case: **no Malaysian government agency will ever ask you to transfer money to any account over the phone.** There is no safe account. No audit requires your balance to be moved.

End the call and contact the agency using a number you look up yourself on their official site. Do not use the number the caller gave you, and do not leave the original call open while you dial — the syndicate can keep that line alive.

If a caller tells you to keep something secret from your family, that alone confirms it is a scam. A real investigation never depends on the victim staying quiet.
